Attack and defence

A second model splits each club’s strength in two: how many goals it would score, and how many it would concede, in a game against an average Premier League side on a neutral pitch. Each number has a range. We’re 80% sure the true value lies inside it. Ranges are wider when there’s less evidence, which mostly means promoted clubs and squads that changed a lot over the summer.

How it works

Before each match, the gap between the two ratings (plus points for the home side) gives an expected result. Afterwards, both clubs move by the difference between what happened and what was expected, multiplied by . Bigger winning margins count for a bit more. Whatever one club gains, the other loses.

Each summer, ratings are pulled of the way back towards 1500. Promoted clubs start at the average rating of the clubs they replace. Only Premier League matches count, from the 2010–11 season onwards, with 2010 seeded from the 2009–10 final table.

The attack and defence ratings come from a separate model. Goals in each match are treated as random, with a rate set by one side’s attack against the other’s defence, plus home advantage. After every match both numbers move towards what happened, and they move more when we’re less sure of them. Every match makes a rating more certain. Each summer the uncertainty goes back up, by more for clubs that lost a large share of their minutes, and those clubs are also pulled further back towards average. The settings were chosen by testing how well the model predicts each match before it’s played. Squad data comes from vaastav/Fantasy-Premier-League.

Player power has two parts. Minutes is the share of the team’s league minutes a player has been on the pitch this season. Quality is a percentile against every Premier League player in the same position, built from per-90 numbers: for goalkeepers, goals prevented, expected goals conceded and FPL’s bonus points system (BPS); for defenders, defensive actions, expected goals conceded with them on the pitch, expected goals and assists, and BPS; for midfielders, expected goals and assists, BPS and defensive actions; for forwards, mostly expected goals and assists. This season is blended with last season (counted at half weight), and players with few minutes are pulled towards the average for their position. Power is minutes × quality, so 100 means a player who never misses a minute and is the best in his position. Injury news comes from the official Fantasy Premier League game.

Match ratings score one game at a time. Each measure (goals + assists, xG + xA, defensive actions, BPS, and goals prevented for goalkeepers) is compared with every appearance in the same position this season, weighted by position, and added up. The total is then scaled so a typical 60+ minute game is a 6.5, and each standard deviation above or below is worth 0.75 of a point, between 3 and 10. Totals are for the match, not per 90, so a ten-minute cameo has less chance to rate highly.

Match forecasts come from both models. Elo turns the gap between two ratings (plus home advantage) into an expected score, then splits it into win, draw and loss, with draws likeliest between evenly matched sides. The goals model gives each side’s expected goals; two Poisson distributions turn those into a probability for every scoreline, and the scorelines are added up into win, draw and loss. The bookmakers’ chances are their odds turned into probabilities and scaled to add up to 100%, which removes their margin (about 3.7% on average). The comparison uses Bet365’s pre-match odds for every match since 2012–13, from football-data.co.uk; each forecast uses only what was known before kick-off.
